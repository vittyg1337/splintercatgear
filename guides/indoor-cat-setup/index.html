Research guide

Build the habitat before the shopping list.

A practical room-by-room framework for giving an indoor cat choice, control, and access to the resources that matter—without buying a houseful of gadgets.

By the Splintercat Research DeskPublished August 30, 20268-minute readEvidence-informed

A good setup is not a pile of cat products. It is a home where a cat can retreat, observe, eat, drink, toilet, scratch, rest, and play without unnecessary competition or surprise.

The framework below is grounded in the AAFP and ISFM environmental-needs guidelines, which organize a healthy feline environment around five ideas: safe places; multiple, separated resources; opportunities for play and predatory behaviour; positive and predictable human interaction; and respect for a cat’s sensory world.

Use this as a planning guide, not a diagnosis.

Sudden changes in eating, drinking, toileting, movement, hiding, or behaviour deserve veterinary attention. Individual cats may need a different setup because of age, mobility, health, history, or relationships with other animals.

1. Observe before rearranging

Spend several ordinary days noticing where your cat already chooses to sleep, watch, scratch, drink, avoid traffic, and interact. Those choices are useful design information.

  • Mark the quiet places your cat already uses.
  • Notice preferred heights, surfaces, and scratching directions.
  • Watch for blocked routes or places one animal can guard.
  • Record which resources are used and which are ignored.

Do not change every variable at once. A small, observable adjustment teaches you more than a complete overnight makeover.

2. Create more than one safe place

A safe place is somewhere a cat can withdraw and feel concealed or protected. It may be elevated, enclosed, quiet, familiar, or some combination of those qualities. A cardboard box, an accessible carrier left open, a covered bed, or a stable perch can all serve the purpose.

As a starting principle, provide at least one cat-sized safe place per cat, and distribute choices so another animal cannot block every entrance. Kittens, senior cats, and cats with limited mobility may need lower options, intermediate steps, ramps, or easier access.

3. Separate key resources

Food, water, toileting, scratching, play, and rest are different activities. Placing everything in one “cat corner” may be efficient for a person, but it removes choice and can create competition.

  • Keep toileting areas away from food and water.
  • Offer water separately from food where the home allows.
  • In multi-cat homes, distribute important resources across locations rather than creating one shared station.
  • Give each cat a way to reach essential resources without passing through a narrow route controlled by another animal.

Separation does not always require another room. Distance, sight-line breaks, furniture, height, and more than one route can all help create distinct stations.

4. Add usable vertical territory

Vertical space can increase the usable territory of a small home, but only when it is stable, reachable, and large enough for the cat using it. Before buying a tower or wall system, measure:

  • the available floor and wall area;
  • the actual usable dimensions of platforms, not the product’s outside dimensions;
  • the route up and down, including safe intermediate steps;
  • clearance from doors, breakables, cords, and unstable furniture;
  • whether the product can be anchored as directed.

A shorter, solid perch beside a useful window may serve a particular cat better than a tall but shaky tower.

5. Put scratching where the behaviour happens

Scratching is normal feline behaviour. Offer stable surfaces in orientations and materials your cat appears to prefer. Useful starting locations often include sleeping areas, room transitions, and places where scratching already occurs.

A vertical post should be tall and steady enough for the cat to stretch and pull without tipping it. Horizontal scratchers should resist sliding. If you are redirecting scratching, place the acceptable option beside the current location before gradually moving it.

6. Design for short hunting sequences

Opportunities to stalk, chase, pounce, catch, and manipulate matter more than a huge toy basket. Rotate a smaller selection, use interactive play, and let the cat complete the sequence by catching the toy periodically.

String, ribbon, feathers, and detachable small parts require supervision and secure storage after play. Adapt intensity and access for kittens, senior cats, and cats with medical or mobility limits.

7. Respect scent, sound, and predictability

Cats use scent to understand familiar territory. Strongly scented cleaners, fragranced litter, sudden object replacement, loud appliances, and constant resource movement can change how secure a space feels.

Introduce new items gradually. When safe and practical, retain familiar bedding or scent-bearing objects during a rearrangement. Put noisy equipment where the cat has another route and somewhere quieter to retreat.

A simple room-map exercise

Draw a rough floor plan. It does not need to be accurate or attractive. Mark the following:

  1. Essential stations: food, water, toileting, rest, scratching, play.
  2. Routes: how the cat moves between them, including vertical routes.
  3. Pressure points: narrow halls, doors, noisy appliances, dog zones, and places another cat can guard.
  4. Unused opportunities: a quiet corner, safe window view, wall area, shelf, or open carrier.

Then choose the smallest change that improves access or choice. Sometimes that is new gear. Sometimes it is moving a bowl, stabilizing a scratcher, opening a route, or repurposing a box you already own.

The Splintercat buying filter

Before purchasing anything, answer five questions:

  • Which observed need does this product address?
  • Where will it go, and what are the exact usable dimensions?
  • Can the intended cat access and use it comfortably?
  • Can it be stabilized, cleaned, maintained, and stored?
  • What will tell you whether it actually helped?

If the first answer is unclear, pause. The best purchase is often the one made after the problem is defined.
