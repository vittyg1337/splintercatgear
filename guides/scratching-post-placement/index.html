Where to put a cat scratching post.

The right scratcher in the wrong place is easy to ignore. Use your cat’s existing behaviour to choose locations, surfaces, and orientations that make sense.

By the Splintercat Research DeskPublished August 30, 20267-minute readEvidence-informed

Put a scratcher where your cat already has a reason to scratch. Useful starting points include beside an existing furniture target, near a preferred sleeping place, and along an important route or room boundary. A scratcher hidden in an unused corner may be convenient for people but irrelevant to the cat.

Scratching is normal behaviour. It stretches the body, conditions the claws, leaves a visible mark, and deposits scent from the paws. The goal is to provide appealing legal surfaces—not to eliminate the behaviour.

Sudden behaviour changes deserve attention.

If scratching changes abruptly or appears alongside pain, reduced movement, hiding, house-soiling, appetite changes, or distress, contact a veterinarian. This guide addresses ordinary environmental setup, not diagnosis.

The best starting locations

Beside the current target

If a cat scratches one arm of the sofa, place an appropriate scratcher directly beside that arm. Do not start across the room. Make the desirable option stable and accessible, protect the furniture temporarily, and reward investigation without forcing the cat’s paws onto the surface.

Near sleeping and resting places

Many cats stretch and scratch after waking. A post or pad beside a frequently used bed, perch, or sofa can meet that sequence at the moment it occurs.

Along routes and visible boundaries

Scratching also communicates. Room entrances, hall transitions, and socially important shared areas can be useful locations, especially when the existing marks appear there. In multi-cat homes, distribute options so one cat cannot control every scratching area.

Match the orientation and material

Look at what the cat already selects. Scratching a sofa arm or door frame suggests a vertical or angled preference. Scratching carpet or a rug may indicate a horizontal preference. Some cats readily use both.

  • Vertical: choose a post tall enough for a comfortable full-body reach and stable enough to resist pulling.
  • Horizontal: choose a pad large enough for the cat to stand on and resistant to sliding.
  • Angled: check that it will not collapse or walk across the floor during use.
  • Material: offer a small number of distinct textures—such as sisal, cardboard, carpet, or wood—and observe the response.

A taller post is not automatically better if the base rocks. Measure the usable scratching surface, base footprint, and available floor area before buying. See the current climbing and scratching watchlist for examples of the specifications we check.

How many scratchers?

There is no universal number that fits every home. The better question is whether each cat has accessible choices in the places where scratching matters. A single post beside the food bowl cannot cover a bedroom wake-up routine, a living-room marking site, and competition between cats.

Start with the observed locations. In a multi-level home, provide options on the levels the cat uses. In a multi-cat home, spread scratchers across territories rather than lining them up as one shared station.

How to redirect furniture scratching

  1. Document the exact site. Note orientation, material, time of day, and what happened immediately before.
  2. Match the function. Put a steady scratcher at that location with a similar orientation.
  3. Make it rewarding. Use play near it, praise voluntary use, or add a small amount of catnip if your cat responds to it.
  4. Protect the old surface. A washable cover or suitable furniture protector can reduce reinforcement while the alternative becomes familiar.
  5. Move slowly, if needed. Once use is consistent, shift the scratcher a short distance at a time. Some locations should remain permanent.

Avoid punishment. It can increase fear and does not teach which surface is acceptable. Never use essential oils or deterrents that may be unsafe for cats.

Placement in a small apartment

Use thin margins of space rather than isolating every cat item in one corner. A narrow post can sit beside a sofa arm; a horizontal pad can slide under a console when not in use; a stable scratcher can form part of a route to a window perch. The small-apartment cat setup guide explains how to separate resources without needing separate rooms.

A five-minute placement check

  • Is the scratcher beside a place or route the cat already uses?
  • Does its orientation match the cat’s observed preference?
  • Can the cat pull hard without tipping or sliding it?
  • Can every cat reach an option without being blocked?
  • Is the surrounding area free of unstable objects and cords?
