Where should you put a litter box in a small apartment?

Choose the calmest always-open location that fits a full-size box, gives your cat a clear route, stays separate from food and water, and remains easy enough to scoop every day.

By the Splintercat Research DeskPublished August 30, 202610-minute readEvidence-informed

In a compact home, the best litter-box location is rarely the most hidden one. It is the place your cat can reach without surprise, conflict, or a closed door—and the place you can clean without moving furniture first.

The AAFP and ISFM environmental-needs guidelines treat toileting as a key resource that should be separated from food, water, rest, scratching, and play. Their house-soiling guidelines add that box size, location, and distribution matter alongside the number of boxes. That turns placement into a fit problem, not a decorating contest.

A setup guide cannot diagnose a toileting change.

If your cat suddenly urinates or defecates outside the box, strains, changes frequency, appears uncomfortable, or stops using a previously accepted box, contact a veterinarian promptly. Review the setup, but do not assume the cause is behavioural.

The quick placement test

A candidate location should pass all six checks below. If it fails one, decide whether the problem can be fixed before placing the box there.

  • Access: the route is open at all times the cat needs it.
  • Calm: no door, appliance, dog, child, or household traffic regularly startles the cat.
  • Exit: the cat can leave without being cornered or blocked by another animal.
  • Separation: the box is meaningfully away from food and water.
  • Fit: the usable box and entry route fit—not merely the product’s outside footprint.
  • Maintenance: you can see, scoop, wash, and refill it without an obstacle course.

Measure the working footprint

Measure the box at its widest and deepest points, then add the space needed for your cat to enter and turn, for you to remove the scoop, and for any mat or waste container. A lid that opens upward, a drawer that pulls forward, or a cabinet door that swings out may require much more room than the listed footprint suggests.

Box size is not a detail to sacrifice automatically. The published AAFP and ISFM house-soiling guidance recommends a rectangular box about one and a half times the cat’s length from nose to the base of the tail. A storage tote or other simple open container can sometimes provide more usable room than a compact product marketed for apartments, provided entry height suits the cat and edges are safe.

How common apartment locations compare

Bathroom

A bathroom can work when its door remains open, the floor is easy to clean, and the box is not squeezed under a vanity or beside a startling fan. Test whether showers create prolonged dampness and whether guests might close the door. “In the bathroom” is not enough; the cat still needs a comfortable approach and exit.

Living-room edge

A quiet wall or sheltered corner can be better than a remote closet because it is visible, ventilated, and easy to maintain. Use furniture or a screen to reduce direct sight lines without enclosing the cat in a dead end. Keep the route clear of speakers, door swings, and the busiest walkway.

Bedroom

A bedroom may offer predictable access and low traffic, especially for a timid cat, but only if daily cleaning makes the arrangement workable for the people sleeping there. Keep the box away from the cat’s feeding and sleeping stations and avoid fragranced masking products.

Closet or cabinet

A closet is suitable only when the entrance cannot close accidentally, air can move, the interior fits the cat and box comfortably, and cleaning remains straightforward. A litter cabinet must have a usable interior—not just attractive outside dimensions. Covered spaces can concentrate odour, so hiding the box never replaces scooping.

Laundry area

Washers, dryers, furnaces, and vents may start without warning. If the only possible location is near an appliance, observe your cat while it runs and preserve another route away. Do not wedge the box where vibration, heat, cords, or stored chemicals create avoidable risk.

A simple studio-apartment plan

  1. Mark food, water, sleep, and the main human traffic route on a rough floor plan.
  2. Circle two possible toileting locations away from the feeding area.
  3. Walk each route at busy and quiet times. Look for doors, bottlenecks, and sudden noise.
  4. Place the box where access and cleaning are most reliable, even if that location is more visible.
  5. Add visual screening only after confirming it does not reduce entry room, airflow, or escape options.

One cat, multiple cats, and multiple locations

The familiar starting rule is one box per cat plus one extra, but professional guidance also emphasizes social relationships and distribution. Two boxes beside each other may function like one station if another cat can guard the route. Spread boxes across genuinely separate locations where possible, and avoid forcing every cat through the same narrow doorway.

A single-cat household may also benefit from a choice of two locations. In a very small apartment, perfect spacing may be impossible; prioritize safe access, cleanliness, and separation from food and water, then discuss persistent problems with your veterinarian.

How to change the location

Abruptly removing a familiar option makes it harder to learn what your cat accepts. When space allows, set up the new box while keeping the established box available. Keep litter type, depth, and cleaning routine consistent at first so location is the main variable. Observe use before removing or moving the familiar box.

Do not punish accidents or carry the cat to the box as a correction. Clean soiled areas appropriately, keep a short log of timing and location, and seek veterinary guidance when the change is new, repeated, or accompanied by other signs.

Placement is only one part of odour control

Ohio State’s Indoor Pet Initiative recommends daily scooping and regular washing with mild detergent. It also notes that some cats avoid fragranced litter or deodorizers. A box hidden in a sealed enclosure can smell acceptable from the room while concentrating odour where the cat must enter.

Build a repeatable loop: keep the scoop nearby, use a closed waste container you empty regularly, protect the floor with a washable mat, and leave enough clearance to wash the entire box.
